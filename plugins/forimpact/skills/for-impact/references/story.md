# Story: altitude, simplicity, the funding rationale, the engagement tool

The Story box of the diagnostic: purpose, what we do made simple, the funding
rationale, and the engagement tool that carries them into a visit. For a full
build, hand off: `altitude-messaging` (the message), `funding-rationale` (the ask
math), `engagement-tool-builder` (the printable tool), `big-bet-concept` (one
transformational ask).

## Altitude

A way to order thinking, messages and conversations. The brain takes meaning
before detail, so start high and let the funder's questions take you down.

| Altitude | Color | Level | The funder's question |
|---|---|---|---|
| **30,000 feet** | Blue | Vision: the why, the purpose, the cause | "Toward what end?" |
| **14,000 feet** | Red | Strategy: the what, the priorities, where the money goes | "Where does the money go?" |
| **3 feet** | Green | Execution: the how, the plan, the specific ask | "How can I help?" |

- **30,000 feet** is the whole picture: what is different if you have all the money
  you need. For Impact also builds three storylines at this altitude to give the
  purpose context (the `altitude-messaging` skill teaches them).
- **14,000 feet** bridges vision to execution: the priorities, the funding model, how
  the pieces connect. Everything is a project, with goals, timelines, a team and a
  budget.
- **3 feet** is where the work happens and where the funder finds their place.

Principles:

- People engage with you at the altitude of your choosing.
- Detail and validation only work after the funder buys in at 30,000 feet. Too many
  organizations try to validate the program before they align on the cause.
- Funders identify with causes and fund programs.
- Use questions to move between altitudes. When a conversation sinks into detail,
  ask a question that lifts it back to the why.
- The colors (blue, red, green) are shorthand teams can use. The diagnostic uses the
  same palette for Story, Prospects and Process, so name the altitude or the box,
  not just the color.

## Simplify: the Rule of Three

- If you have ten things to say, find the three that matter. If you have six
  priorities, lead with three.
- People hold three things well. Use three for priorities, key messages, ways to
  help and funding levels.
- Three is a strong default, not a law. Use another number when the content truly
  has it.
- The test for "What does your organization do?": say only what it takes for the
  listener to say, "I get it. I get what you do."

## The funding rationale

The ability to link the big impact to what specific dollars will do. Many
organizations cannot say what $1M, or $10K, actually accomplishes.

- A strong rationale connects dollars to a specific impact ("$X to achieve Y"),
  fits on a napkin, and makes it easy for the funder to see where they fit.
- It is about the impact of the funds, not about restricting them.
- Start from the guidepost: the initiative that frames every ask, with an ownable
  destination, a funding goal, a time horizon and measurable gains. The per-unit
  number ("$1,000 covers one trainee for a year") is the entry point inside it, not
  the whole case.
- Before: "Give $1,000 and get your name on a brick." After: "$1,000 closes the gap
  for three trainees, and each one serves a community for a year."

`funding-rationale`.

## The engagement tool

A simple visual map that presents the case for support in a shoulder-to-shoulder
visit. It is an engagement tool, not a presentation tool.

What it does:

1. It engages the prospect in conversation and dialogue.
2. It gives the visit its framework and flow.
3. It puts the close (how you can help) in front of both of you.

What it is:

- Built to be used together in the visit. It is not a brochure, a collateral piece
  or a text-heavy document, and it does not stand on its own.
- Usually one page, organized at altitude, with prompts, framing devices and white
  space to write in during the conversation.
- It moves from 30,000 feet down to the specific ways to help.
- Forms include a napkin drawing, a vision card, a large engagement poster and a
  one-page case summary.
- The best message begins in the prospect's head. Use their words when you can.
